How sure

  • The top 5 hold 41% of hours in this sample. With only 36 people, resampling gives a range of 32% to 52%.
  • The top 5 total 2,099 hours of 5,124. The slider is an input, not a forecast.
Where the hours come from: injuries and musculoskeletal problems lose the most hours. Medical and dental visits are the most frequent, but short.

How sure

  • Chance of seeing this by luck (random shuffle of 36 employees, 20,000 tries): under 1 in 100.
  • Still holds when each person's single longest absence is removed: top 5 give 31% of later hours. So it is a repeating pattern, not one long illness.
  • Same answer for number of events, not only hours. Employees with no records in a period count as zero.
Why: not who they are. Age, commute, years at the company, children, BMI, travel cost and workload do not separate heavy from light absentees.

Honest reading

  • No trait is clearly linked (smallest p-value 0.39, with 7 traits tested). Treat this as "no evidence", not "proof of no effect": 36 people is small.
  • Planned care is short by nature, so the 93% is expected (95% range 86% to 96% lower). Logistic regression on 696 events, errors clustered by employee.
When: Mondays. They hold 22% of absences but 29% of hours: Monday absences run longer. Thursday is the lightest.

How sure

  • Chance of a Monday share this high by luck (weekday shuffled within each person): under 1 in 100.
  • Without the top 5 people, Mondays are still the largest day but smaller: 26% of hours.
  • Months and seasons also differ, but with 3 years and 36 people two long absences can create a peak, so I do not build on them.
Fix: aim at the people, not at everyone. A company-wide attendance policy spreads effort over the many people who cause little of the loss.

1. Support the repeat group

Each quarter, list the people whose hours were highest in the last two years and offer a private check-in and support plan. The top 5 are 41% of hours.

Who tab

2. Pilot Monday cover

Mondays carry 29% of hours. Trial a Monday return-to-work call or a shift swap for 8 weeks and compare with the other days.

When tab

3. Make planned care easy to book

54% of events are visits. Bookable slots turn surprise gaps into planned ones.

Where tab

4. Prove it on your own data

Repeat the same-people test on your own roster before acting. 36 couriers is a starting point, not a verdict.

Why the groups are weak

Limits

  • 36 people and 740 events. Differences are patterns, not proof of cause.
  • A Brazilian courier company, 2007-2010. Real data, not a call center, no attrition data.
  • 44 events have 0 recorded hours (kept in counts, dropped from the long-absence model).
  • The cost per hour is an assumption you can change. The split into 2007-08 and 2009-10 is by calendar year taken from record order, because the file has no dates.
